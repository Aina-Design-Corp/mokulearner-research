Localizing cross-domain metrics for Hawaiʻi

Bridging the gap between federal county-level baselines and the 33 traditional moku districts where community governance actually happens. When a community measures its water or soil, that reading should count — and stay traceable to who gathered it, where, and when.

Hawaiʻi has 5 counties but 33 moku districts. Federal baseline indicators — farm counts, unemployment rates, median income, rainfall, energy production — are published at county resolution through the Data Commons knowledge graph. The Mokunet platform presents these as island-effect baselines: they tell you where things stand at the island level. Research gaps often occur within the island model.

This repository is where Hawaiʻi researchers and community groups contribute environmental observations and community statistics to a shared research commons, sharpening the picture of what's actually happening in their moku. A water quality study in Maunalua Bay and a food security survey in Waiʻanae become comparable side by side — making district-level variation visible for the first time.

Two pathways into the commons

Environmental observations observation

Water quality, soil tests, species surveys, air monitoring — samples that show what conditions actually are at specific places. Each dataset carries a sample_context block describing what was physically collected: soil, water, sediment, air, tissue, sludge, or mixed matrices under one topic.

Community indicators indicator

Demographics, employment, wellbeing surveys, infrastructure — sub-county statistics that refine the county-level federal baselines at the moku level. Each dataset declares which Data Commons variables it supplements, and needs coordinates or explicit moku IDs so refinements resolve spatially.

Spatial overlays spatial_overlay

GIS layers — wetland boundaries, land use classification, parcel boundaries — that enhance place coverage on the shared map. Typically contributed as GeoJSON.

Both research pathways resolve to specific moku districts and connect to shared sustainability goals. When your data includes coordinates, the platform anchors it to the right district automatically; observation data without coordinates needs only a text description of geographic scope.

How to contribute

  1. Register as a contributor (GitHub issue)
  2. Fork the repository — this creates your own copy to edit
  3. Add your data to contributions/{your-slug}/{dataset-slug}/
  4. Include a metadata.json (use the template)
  5. Open a pull request — GitHub Actions validates automatically

If your lab already uses Git for code, the same workflow applies to data. The full step-by-step guide is in CONTRIBUTING.md.

How contributions join the shared map

Contributed datasets become part of the Mokunet shared map with their provenance intact — who gathered each record, where, and when. Geocoded observations are anchored to their moku and to location cells on the shared map, then tied to the UN Sustainable Development Goals they measure. Indicator datasets additionally track exactly which county baselines they refine. Both contribution types land on the same shared map, which is what enables cross-domain comparison at the district level.

The platform recognizes 33 traditional moku districts across 7 islands. You don't need to know these — the system assigns moku from coordinates automatically. If you're curious, see the moku districts reference.
